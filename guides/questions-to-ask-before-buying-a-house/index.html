Buyer's guide

Questions to ask before buying a house.

The right questions surface what the listing leaves out. Some are for your agent and the seller — but a surprising number you can answer yourself from the public record before you ever make an offer.

Check an address now

A house is easy to inspect. The street around it is not — and it's the part you can't renovate. These are the questions worth answering before you commit, grouped by who can answer them.

Questions the public record can answer today

You don't need to wait for the seller for these — they're in open municipal data, and a tool like Agent Neighbor pulls them by address:

  • What gets reported on this block? A pattern of noise, sanitation or dumping complaints tells you how the street actually lives.
  • Has the property or its neighbors been cited? Code and maintenance violations point to deferred upkeep or absentee owners.
  • What's being built nearby? Permits reveal upcoming construction, demolitions and change on the block.
  • Is it in a flood zone? A FEMA flood-zone designation affects insurance costs and risk — check it before, not after.

Questions for your agent

  • How long has it been on the market, and have there been price cuts?
  • What have comparable homes on this block actually sold for?
  • Are there any known plans — zoning changes, developments, road work — nearby?
  • What do the last twelve months of activity on this street look like?

Questions for the seller

  • What work has been done to the house, and was it permitted?
  • Have there been any recurring issues — water, pests, heating, drainage?
  • Why are you selling, and how long have you owned it?
  • Are there disclosures about the property or the neighborhood you should share?

Questions for your inspector

  • Does the visible work match the permit history, or is there unpermitted work?
  • What are the near-term maintenance costs — roof, HVAC, systems?
  • Any signs of past water intrusion or structural movement?

Put the block on the same footing as the house

Buyers spend hours on the building and almost none on the street. Flip that ratio a little: read the block's public record and its permit history before you write an offer. It's free, it's fast, and it's the context that makes every other question sharper.
