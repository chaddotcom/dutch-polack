Buyer's guide

How to research a neighborhood before you buy.

The listing sells you the house. Nobody hands you the block. Here's how to read a street's public record — 311 complaints, code violations, permits, schools and environmental risk — before you make an offer.

Score an address now

You spend hours on the house — the kitchen, the roof, the comps — and almost none on the thing you can't renovate: the street it sits on. The good news is that most of what shapes a block is a matter of public record, and it's free to read.

1. Start with the block, not the building

A home inspection covers the property line inward. But noise, dumping, traffic complaints, nearby demolition and code enforcement all happen around the house, and they follow you after closing. Before you fall for the finishes, spend twenty minutes on the block.

2. Read the 311 record

311 is the non-emergency line residents use to report problems — noise, illegal dumping, broken streetlights, abandoned vehicles, sanitation. Most major cities publish every request as open data. What you're looking for is pattern, not the odd one-off: a steady stream of the same complaint on one block tells you how the street actually lives.

3. Check code violations and enforcement

Code violations show where the city has cited a property for maintenance, safety or zoning problems. Repeat violations at a specific address are a flag to raise with your agent. A cluster across the block can signal absentee landlords or deferred upkeep nearby.

4. Look at building permits

Permits are a leading indicator. A rise in renovation and new-construction permits often means a block on the upswing — but it can also mean years of scaffolding, dust and street closures next door. Either way, you want to know before, not after.

5. Don't skip schools, environment and response

  • Schools: find the schools the address is actually zoned for, and check whether those boundaries have been stable.
  • Environmental risk: flood zone status, heat and air quality are all mapped by public agencies like FEMA and the EPA.
  • Fire and emergency response: incident history and station proximity give a sense of coverage for the area.

6. Turn it into one view

Chasing each of these across a dozen city portals is slow, and it's easy to miss one. Agent Neighbor reads these public feeds for an address in your browser and turns them into a single Street Context Index — with every underlying record listed and linked, so you can open it up and check the math instead of trusting a black box.

A word on what the data can and can't do

Public records are factual but incomplete: some problems are never reported, and cities publish on their own schedules. Use this research to decide what to look into — then confirm anything that matters with a proper inspection and your own professionals. It's a map, not a verdict.
